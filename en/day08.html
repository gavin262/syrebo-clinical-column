Issue 08

Upper-limb impairment after stroke: a 60-person randomized study of innovative mirror therapy

From the paper

创新式镜像疗法对脑卒中患者上肢功能障碍的康复效果

陈涛、刘沙鑫、陈雪莲、毛磊、李德波、甘林玉

新医学, 2024, 55(6): 397-402

DOI: 10.3969/j.issn.0253-9802.2024.06.001

View source

SY-HR08E mirror training scene with screen, host and gloves (official photo)

Conventional mirror therapy uses a mirror to suggest that the affected hand is moving. The innovative version asks a robotic glove to carry the affected hand through the same action the mirror shows, so seeing and doing share the session. In 2024 New Medicine published a randomized study by Chen and colleagues that compared the two versions in 60 people over four weeks. The paper reports larger gains for innovative mirror therapy on upper-limb movement, wrist-hand movement, and daily activities.

Background

Mirror therapy has a known limit: if the affected hand does not move, the visual suggestion has no paired motor input. Connecting assisted flexion and extension to the mirror task is an attempt to have the affected side actually complete a movement inside a range the therapist sets.

Whether that change shows up on scores has to be tested against conventional mirror therapy, not against doing nothing. Sixty people, split evenly, is a preliminary randomized size.

Study overview

A training image on the journal page shows a Yisheng mark. The column maps that mark to the SY-HR08E in official materials and shows a product photograph with screen, console, and glove. The mapping is an identification note, not a model claim printed as a trial registration.

Study design

Random-number allocation. Innovative mirror therapy, combining a mirror task with a robotic glove, was compared with conventional mirror therapy. Outcomes included FMA-UE, FMA-WH, and the modified Barthel Index.

Sample and schedule

Sixty people with upper-limb impairment after stroke, 30 in each group, for four weeks.

Main findings

The paper reports greater improvement for innovative mirror therapy on FMA-UE, FMA-WH, and the modified Barthel Index. That is the study’s between-group description. Means and P values are not recalculated on this page; where the journal page is still being checked, those figures stay with the paper.

What this study adds

The question is whether adding an assisted movement that matches the mirror changes scores relative to the mirror alone. Sixty people provide a preliminary contrast, not a home-program proof.

Linking the journal image to the SY-HR08E only helps a reader recognize the device. Whether the score difference is stable waits on an independent check of the full numbers.
