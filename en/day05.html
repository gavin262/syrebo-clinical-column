Issue 05

Hand function in early subacute stroke: a single-blind pilot RCT of a Syrebo soft robotic glove

From the paper

Feasibility and preliminary efficacy of a soft robotic glove for hand rehabilitation in early subacute stroke: a pilot investigation

Lin Y, Wang C, Yin G, et al.

BioMedical Engineering OnLine, 2026

DOI: 10.1186/s12938-026-01614-6 · ChiCTR2000034614

View source

In the early subacute phase the first question is not how many points a score might rise. It is whether the sessions can be finished, and whether they are safe. A research team published a single-blind pilot randomized trial in BioMedical Engineering OnLine on the Syrebo SY-HR03E for hand rehabilitation in that window. The paper reports that all 20 participants finished four weeks, with no dropouts and no adverse events during the study. The paper reports that Fugl–Meyer hand scores improved in both groups, with a larger change in the glove group. A within-group change in daily activities reached statistical significance in the glove group.

Background

The arm and hand usually recover later than walking. Eating, dressing, and washing depend on fractionated finger movement, not on one strength number. Conventional hand therapy helps, but the number of repetitions a therapist can deliver is limited, and the same movement is hard to repeat at the bedside.

A pneumatic glove can flex and extend the fingers with little mass on the hand, which is why bedside use is discussed. People in the early subacute phase are also recovering spontaneously. Any “preliminary effect” has to be read against that background. A pilot randomized trial is there to show that a protocol is feasible and to leave a signal for a larger study. It does not replace a confirmatory trial.

Study overview

The paper describes the SY-HR03E as a commercial soft glove plus a tabletop controller. Reported mass is about 3.5 kg for the system and about 110 g for the glove. Pneumatic bellows on the back of the hand assist flexion and extension. Pressure sensors watch the change produced when the person tries to move; above a preset threshold of 0.7 kPa the controller treats that change as movement intention and inflates or deflates. The intended mode is active-assist, not a fully passive pull.

Study design

Single-blind pilot randomization. The robotic group added glove-assisted opening and closing to standard rehabilitation. The conventional group received traditional hand therapy of the same session length. Both groups also received one hour a day of standard rehabilitation that excluded additional hand therapy. A therapist supervised posture. Registry: ChiCTR2000034614.

Sample and schedule

Twenty inpatients, 7 days to 3 months after onset, 10 in each group. Glove sessions were 30 minutes a day, five days a week, for four weeks. Each open-close was held for about three seconds. One author listed an affiliation with the Syrebo company; the paper states that the authors declared no competing interests. That relationship belongs in the reading.

Main findings

The paper reports that all 20 people completed four weeks, with no dropouts and no adverse events during the study. Hand Fugl-Meyer scores improved in both groups, and the authors reported a group-by-time interaction for hand function and the modified Barthel Index. On the modified Barthel Index, only the robotic group’s within-group change is reported as statistically significant (p<0.001); the conventional group’s within-group change is not. Hand Brunnstrom stage likewise showed a significant within-group change only in the robotic group. In a 10-versus-10 pilot, a within-group P value is a signal, not evidence that daily function will rise for patients in general.

What this study adds

Many soft-glove studies enroll people in the chronic phase. This pilot moves the feasibility and safety question into the window from 7 days to 3 months. It does not move chronic-phase efficacy claims earlier.

Hand scores and daily-activity scores did not take the same statistical shape: the paper reports a significant within-group Barthel change only in the glove group. That split is worth testing again. It is not a statement that activities of daily living have been solved. A 110 g glove and intent detection make bedside and, later, home discussion possible. This study itself was done with inpatients.

Source

Limits Pilot randomization, 10 per group. Preliminary signals need a larger sample. The P value is the paper’s report for that outcome. A device-related affiliation is disclosed and should be weighed.

Source: Lin Y, Wang C, Yin G, et al. Feasibility and preliminary efficacy of a soft robotic glove for hand rehabilitation in early subacute stroke: a pilot investigation. BioMedical Engineering OnLine, 2026. DOI: 10.1186/s12938-026-01614-6 (Clinical Trial Registry: ChiCTR2000034614).
