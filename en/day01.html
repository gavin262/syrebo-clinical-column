Issue 01

Hand function in acute stroke: a randomized trial of robot-assisted training

From the paper

Effects of robotic hand-assisted rehabilitation on motor function and daily living activities in acute stroke: a randomized controlled trial

Sunnetci MA, Menek B

Scientific Reports, 2026

DOI: 10.1038/s41598-025-32258-6 · NCT06805955

View source

After stroke, the hand often returns to daily tasks more slowly than walking: a cup, a button, or a utensil becomes a specific obstacle. The acute window changes quickly, so anything added there needs a randomized comparison rather than a case impression. In 2026 Scientific Reports published a randomized trial that added Syrebo robot-assisted hand training to conventional neurodevelopmental therapy. The paper reports greater gains on dexterity, bimanual activities, and self-care than therapy alone. The sample is 30 people, and spasticity did not separate the groups.

Background

The hand is difficult because it needs force, fractionated movement, and actual use. Gait can be practiced as one target; the hand has to grasp and release inside an unsteady posture. Neurodevelopmental therapy is already the base of acute rehabilitation, but a therapist cannot repeat every grasp indefinitely, so dose and consistency are hard to stretch.

A soft robotic glove can, in principle, supply those repetitions under supervision. Acute patients are still changing. Whether the device can be added safely, and whether functional scores move with it, is a controlled question. This trial addresses that stage rather than repeating a chronic-phase study.

Study overview

The paper names the device as a SYREBO robotic hand but does not specify a product model. The photograph illustrates a Syrebo system; its correspondence to the study device is not confirmed.

Study design

A single-center randomized comparison. One group received robot-assisted hand training plus neurodevelopmental therapy (NT). The control group received NT alone. The outcomes were fine dexterity, bimanual daily activities, and self-care, not strength alone.

Sample and schedule

The study enrolled and randomized 30 people in the acute phase. Both groups followed an eight-week program. The paper describes an additional 25 minutes of robotic hand therapy alongside neurodevelopmental therapy and says the robotic-training group received more total treatment time. The trial registry instead describes 60 minutes per session for both groups, with 35 minutes of neurodevelopmental therapy plus 25 minutes of robotic training for the robotic group. This difference requires clarification. Spasticity measures were collected as well.

Main findings

The paper reports larger improvements in the robot-assisted group on the Nine-Hole Peg Test, the ABILHAND questionnaire, and the Barthel Index. That is the paper’s between-group finding for the programs studied in this sample. The trial registry describes the between-group results differently for some measures, including ABILHAND, so these descriptions require clarification. The paper also reports longer total treatment time for the robotic-training group; the difference cannot be attributed to the device alone. It is not a promised recovery speed. Spasticity did not show a significant between-group difference, so not every measure moved apart.

What this study adds

The useful part is the timing: robot-assisted practice was tested in the acute phase against ongoing neurodevelopmental therapy, rather than borrowed from chronic-phase results. The paper reports changes in several functional measures; the differences from the registry description need clarification.

Thirty randomized patients can only support a cautious reading. The trial does not answer long-term home use, and it does not show that every stroke subtype follows the same path. The treating clinician still sets the plan.

Source

Limits Small sample (n=30). The paper reports different total treatment times, whereas the registry describes equal session lengths. Baseline NHPT and MAS differed between groups; the authors reported an adjusted analysis. The trial was registered retrospectively, and the same physiotherapist delivered treatment and performed assessments. There was no long-term follow-up and no significant between-group difference on spasticity. Score changes are the study’s report, not an individual promise.

Source: Sunnetci MA, Menek B. Effects of robotic hand-assisted rehabilitation on motor function and daily living activities in acute stroke: a randomized controlled trial. Scientific Reports, 2026. DOI: 10.1038/s41598-025-32258-6 (ClinicalTrials.gov: NCT06805955).
