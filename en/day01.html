Issue 01

Hand function in acute stroke: a randomized trial of robot-assisted training at NP Brain Hospital

From the paper

Effects of robotic hand-assisted rehabilitation on motor function and daily living activities in acute stroke: a randomized controlled trial

Sunnetci MA, Menek B

Scientific Reports, 2026

DOI: 10.1038/s41598-025-32258-6 · NCT06805955

View source

Syrebo soft rehabilitation glove + main unit + tablet training system (product photo)

After stroke, the hand often returns to daily tasks more slowly than walking: a cup, a button, or a utensil becomes a specific obstacle. The acute window changes quickly, so anything added there needs a randomized comparison rather than a case impression. In 2026 Scientific Reports published a randomized trial from NP Brain Hospital in Turkey that added Syrebo robot-assisted hand training to conventional neurodevelopmental therapy. The paper reports greater gains on dexterity, bimanual activities, and self-care than therapy alone. The sample is 30 people, and spasticity did not separate the groups.

Background

The hand is difficult because it needs force, fractionated movement, and actual use. Gait can be practiced as one target; the hand has to grasp and release inside an unsteady posture. Neurodevelopmental therapy is already the base of acute rehabilitation, but a therapist cannot repeat every grasp indefinitely, so dose and consistency are hard to stretch.

A soft robotic glove can, in principle, supply those repetitions under supervision. Acute patients are still changing. Whether the device can be added safely, and whether functional scores move with it, is a controlled question. This trial addresses that stage rather than repeating a chronic-phase study.

Study overview

The paper names the device as a SYREBO robotic hand. The photograph shows a soft glove, console, and tablet so readers can see the form factor. Model details belong to the paper and the clinical protocol.

Study design

A single-center randomized comparison. One group received robot-assisted hand training plus neurodevelopmental therapy (NT). The control group received NT alone. The outcomes were fine dexterity, bimanual daily activities, and self-care, not strength alone.

Sample and schedule

NP Brain Hospital enrolled 30 people in the acute phase and randomized them. Robot-assisted sessions were three times a week for eight weeks. Spasticity measures were collected as well.

Main findings

The paper reports larger improvements in the robot-assisted group on the Nine-Hole Peg Test, the ABILHAND questionnaire, and the Barthel Index. That is a between-group finding in this sample. It is not a promised recovery speed. Spasticity did not show a significant between-group difference, so not every measure moved apart.

What this study adds

The useful part is the timing: robot-assisted practice was tested in the acute phase against ongoing neurodevelopmental therapy, rather than borrowed from chronic-phase results. Three functional measures moving together is more informative than a single score.

Thirty randomized patients can only support a cautious reading. The trial does not answer long-term home use, and it does not show that every stroke subtype follows the same path. The treating clinician still sets the plan.

Source

Limits Small sample (n=30). No significant between-group difference on spasticity. Score changes are the study’s report, not an individual promise.

Source: Sunnetci MA, Menek B. Effects of robotic hand-assisted rehabilitation on motor function and daily living activities in acute stroke: a randomized controlled trial. Scientific Reports, 2026. DOI: 10.1038/s41598-025-32258-6 (ClinicalTrials.gov: NCT06805955).
