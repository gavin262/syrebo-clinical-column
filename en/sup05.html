Supplement 05

A soft glove and virtual scenes: brain activation in 20 healthy adults and 20 patients, not an efficacy trial

From the paper

Brain Activation Pattern Caused by Soft Rehabilitation Glove and Virtual Reality Scenes: A Pilot fNIRS Study

P. Liu, X. Yang, F. Han, G. Peng, Q. Li, L. Huang, L. Wang, Y. Fan

IEEE Transactions on Neural Systems and Rehabilitation Engineering, 2024;32:3848–3857

DOI: 10.1109/TNSRE.2024.3482470 · PMID: 39418155

View source

Paper figures: reuse not confirmed; none are reproduced on this page.

This is a recording of brain activation, not a trial of clinical function. Twenty healthy adults and twenty people after stroke completed three tasks: passive grasp assisted by a robotic glove, watching a virtual grasp, and the glove together with the video. The abstract reports wider cortical activation for the video and for the combined task. Wider activation is not recovered function. The brand and model have not been checked in the methods, so the paper is not counted as a confirmed device experiment.

Background

Near-infrared spectroscopy looks at cortical oxygenation while a task is happening. It answers which regions are more active during a movement or a video. It does not answer whether a hand score is still different weeks later.

Differences between healthy adults and patients can also come from effort, attention, and compensation. Those readings stay available. The abstract is not collapsed into “more activation is better.”

Study overview

The three tasks were glove-assisted passive grasp, watching a grasp in a virtual scene, and both together. Recordings covered prefrontal cortex, motor cortex, and occipital cortex, with activation and functional connectivity. The question is brain activity under different sensory conditions, not Fugl-Meyer or the Action Research Arm Test after a course of sessions. This page does not show a product photograph, because the model is unchecked and another device’s picture would stand in for it.

Study design

A pilot near-infrared experiment with three controlled tasks, comparing healthy adults and people after stroke. Clinical recovery was not the primary outcome, and the abstract does not confirm a glove brand.

Sample and schedule

Twenty healthy adults and twenty people after stroke, 40 in total. That is the number recorded during tasks, not the number who finished a treatment course.

Main findings

The abstract reports wider cortical activation in both groups during the video and the combined task. Mean cortical activation across the three tasks was higher in the group after stroke than in the healthy group, especially in prefrontal cortex, with the abstract’s P<0.05. Functional connectivity was weaker in most regions after stroke and stronger in some right-hemisphere regions. The authors read multisensory stimulation as a possible route to more communication among regions. That is a statement about activation and connectivity.

What this study adds

For program design, the study is a clue to how passive movement plus a visual scene changes activation during the task. It supports further mechanism work on multisensory practice. It does not support writing that activation as functional recovery or as a long-term result, and it does not support naming a specific product before the model is checked.

Source

Limits A pilot mechanism study. Clinical recovery was not the primary outcome. Activation differences can reflect compensation and should not be read as “higher is better.” Brand and model remain unchecked, so this is not a confirmed device experiment.
