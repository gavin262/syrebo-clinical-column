Issue 04

Grip and motor scores after stroke: a randomized trial of robotic training gloves

From the paper

Efficacy of robotic training gloves in improving hand function and movement in stroke patients

Abd Elhady AS, Ahmed GM, Hassan A, Ibrahim SM, Abdelmageed SM

SPORT TK-EuroAmerican Journal of Sport Sciences, 2025, 14, Article 40

DOI: 10.6018/sportk.660321

View source

Generic soft rehabilitation glove illustration; the source does not specify a model, so no model correspondence is claimed

After stroke, clinicians often split the hand into two practical problems: whether it can grip, and whether it can move with control. A 2025 randomized trial in SPORT TK-EuroAmerican Journal of Sport Sciences measured both. The paper reports statistically significant between-group mean differences favoring robotic training gloves on grip and on the Fugl-Meyer motor score. Thirty people took part, and the paper does not name a commercial model.

Background

A change in grip need not produce a usable movement, and a higher motor score need not mean a person can lift a cup. Measuring force and movement in the same randomized comparison avoids publishing only the kinder of the two.

Glove papers often omit the model. Without a model, a result cannot be copied onto a product specification. This summary therefore stays with the contrasts the paper actually gives.

Study overview

Study design

Randomized comparison of robotic training-glove practice against a control condition. Shared outcomes were grip and the Fugl-Meyer motor score, reported as mean differences with 95% confidence intervals.

Sample and schedule

Thirty people after stroke, 15 in each group. The paper does not identify a marketed model. The generic glove photograph is an illustration, not a device identification.

Main findings

The reported between-group mean differences are grip 1.87 (95% CI 0.39 to 3.35, P=0.015) and Fugl-Meyer 16.67 (95% CI 3.9 to 29.4, P=0.012). A mean difference is not the gain of each person. The motor-score interval is wide, which is what a small sample looks like when the estimate is imprecise.

What this study adds

Publishing both the difference and the interval is more usable than the sentence “better than control.” The direction is visible, and so is the uncertainty.

Because no model is named, the result cannot be retold as a specification or an indication for a particular Syrebo glove. Thirty people also leave room for sampling noise.

Source

Limits Small sample (n=30). No model is identified, and the photo is not a match. Mean differences and P values are the study’s statistics.

Source: Abd Elhady AS, Ahmed GM, Hassan A, Ibrahim SM, Abdelmageed SM. Efficacy of robotic training gloves in improving hand function and movement in stroke patients. SPORT TK-EuroAmerican Journal of Sport Sciences, 2025, 14, Article 40. DOI: 10.6018/sportk.660321.
