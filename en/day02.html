Issue 02

Post-stroke hand function: a single-blind RCT of a soft robotic glove and mirror therapy, with 6-month follow-up

From the paper

Soft robotic gloves versus mirror therapy: a long-term comparative study on hand function and motor recovery in post-stroke rehabilitation

Abdelraouf OR, et al.

Journal of Rehabilitation Medicine, 2025

DOI: 10.2340/jrm.v57.43482 · PMID: 40874348 · NCT05898971 · PMCID: PMC12406746

Mirror therapy uses visual input to recruit the affected side. A soft robotic glove uses compliant actuation to repeat opening and closing. Which change lasts longer cannot be answered at the last training day. A 2025 single-blind randomized trial in the Journal of Rehabilitation Medicine compared the two approaches for eight weeks and followed participants for six months after the intervention. The paper reports statistically significant between-group gains for the glove on the Box and Block Test, grip strength, and FMA-UE at the end of training, and reports that those glove-group gains were still present at six months.

Background

The question that matters after hand training is whether the change is still there during follow-up. Mirror therapy is simple and depends on attention to the reflected limb. A glove shifts repetition onto an external actuator, with a different demand on attention and endurance. Both are rehabilitation methods. This is not a ranking of brands.

A score on the final training day can confuse a short stimulus with a stable change. A randomized comparison with a six-month visit is what makes that distinction possible.

Study overview

The paper identifies Shanghai SiYi Intelligent Technology as the glove manufacturer, the company behind Syrebo. It does not specify a product model. Whether the column photograph shows the study device has not been confirmed.

Study design

In this single-blind randomized comparison, both groups first received conventional physical therapy, followed by either soft robotic glove training or mirror therapy. Conventional therapy continued during the six-month follow-up. Outcomes were grasp-and-release dexterity (Box and Block), grip strength, and upper-limb movement (FMA-UE).

Sample and schedule

The abstract describes 49 randomly assigned participants. The study flow description reports 54 randomized and 49 completing follow-up and included in the analysis. Other screening and enrollment counts in the paper differ, and parts of the flowchart also require clarification. Training lasted eight weeks. The registry entry is NCT05898971.

Main findings

At the end of training the paper reports between-group differences favoring the glove: Box and Block P=0.004, grip P=0.011, FMA-UE P=0.021. At six months the glove group’s gains were still present. Those P values describe this sample. They do not assign a recovery size to every patient.

What this study adds

The extra information is time. The authors report that the between-group difference between these rehabilitation programs remained at the follow-up visit, while conventional therapy continued. That offers clinicians a longer view than a comparison on the last training day.

Forty-nine people is still a small trial. Baseline differences, lesion side, and practice during follow-up can all move a six-month score. The finding belongs to this population and this contrast.

Source
