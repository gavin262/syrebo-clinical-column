Finger robots intended for home use: a development note in the Journal of the Robotics Society of Japan

From the paper

古館裕大、千葉馨、石田裕二、三上貞芳

日本ロボット学会誌, 2023; 41(4)

DOI: 10.7210/jrsj.41.331

View source

Sharma et al., Cureus 2024, Figure 2: Syrebo soft robotic glove and console (CC BY 4.0). From that case report, not a figure in the Japanese review, which does not show the glove. 10.7759/cureus.59314

Home hand rehabilitation has been discussed for years. Commercialized examples in the society literature are still few. In 2023 the Journal of the Robotics Society of Japan published an article by Furudate and colleagues on a finger-rehabilitation robot intended for home use after hemiparesis, and on developing it with clinicians. The authors note that commercialized home-use cases remain rare, and they name the Syrebo hand glove as one such case. The article is a development and collaboration note, not an efficacy trial.

Background

Between the ward and the home sit donning, safety limits, and use when no therapist is in the room. Engineering papers put those constraints inside the work with clinicians. Being named means the authors chose this product as an example. It does not mean the society ran a controlled study.

“Few have been commercialized” is also a limit. Reading it as “home rehabilitation is already generally available” turns an example into an epidemiologic claim.

Study overview

The article does not print a glove photograph. The glove-and-console image in this column is Figure 2 of the 2024 Cureus case report by Sharma and colleagues (CC BY 4.0). The caption says so. It is not the original figure of the Japanese society paper.

Study design

A society-journal development article about finger robots for home use and about working with clinicians. There is no randomization and no between-group functional comparison.

Sample and schedule

A clinical sample size does not apply. The article discusses a path to a product, not outcomes in a cohort.

Main findings

The statement, as used here, is that commercialized finger robots aimed at the home are still few, and that Syrebo is one example the authors name. The column keeps that scope. It is not rewritten as efficacy, as a market rank, or as a promise that training at home will be completed.

What this study adds

The note’s value is that home use is treated as an engineering problem defined with clinicians. A commercialized example shows that one device moved from prototype to product.

Whether it can be used safely and repeatedly at home still depends on indication, supervision, and separate clinical studies. The society paper does not provide those data.
